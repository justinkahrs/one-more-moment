When someone you love has cancer, you may want to make the holidays easier or more enjoyable but feel unsure about what would actually help. Should you bring dinner, plan a visit, buy a gift, or offer to handle the shopping?

If you are wondering how to help an adult with cancer during the holidays, start by asking what they want and offering one specific way to reduce stress. The most useful support leaves room for their preferences and gives them fewer things to manage.

You do not need a large budget or a perfect plan. A grocery pickup, a favorite movie, or a ride arranged in advance can make a meaningful difference.

## Ask Before Planning

Energy, treatment schedules, mobility, appetite, immunity, and comfort can vary. A large gathering that sounds enjoyable to you may feel tiring to someone else. A person may also want to keep a familiar tradition rather than have everyone change their plans around them.

Try a question that offers a real choice:

- "Would a short visit on Saturday feel good, or would you prefer a phone call?"
- "Would you like help putting up a few decorations?"
- "Would dinner together be enjoyable, or would a meal dropped off be easier?"
- "Is there anything you would especially like to do this holiday season?"

Let a no be a complete answer, and make it easy to change plans. Ask before inviting additional guests, arranging a surprise, or sharing personal updates with others. For questions about visitors, food, travel, or infection precautions, follow the person's care team's guidance.

## Help With Practical Things

Holiday support for cancer patients often starts with everyday tasks. A specific offer can be easier to answer than "Let me know if you need anything."

Consider offering to:

- Deliver a meal at an agreed time, after checking food preferences and restrictions.
- Pick up groceries from a list or arrange a delivery they approve.
- Provide transportation to an appointment or holiday activity.
- Run an errand, return a package, or collect an order.
- Wrap gifts or help with shopping within their budget.
- Put up simple decorations and return to take them down.
- Coordinate visitors so the person does not have to manage every message.
- Handle a small household task, such as dishes or taking out the trash.
- Organize a family gathering, including setup and cleanup.

Make the offer manageable: "I'm going to the store Thursday. Could I pick up your groceries and leave them at the door at 4?" Agree on the details, follow through, and avoid creating an expectation that the person needs to host you.

## Create Meaningful Time Together

Support can also mean making space for something the person enjoys. Ask about their interests beyond cancer, and let them decide whether they want to talk about treatment or something entirely different.

Ideas might include a favorite movie night, a family dinner, a quiet afternoon together, a visit to a favorite restaurant, a small gathering, a local experience, or revisiting a familiar tradition. A short drive to see holiday lights may be enjoyable if the person wants to go and can comfortably participate.

Keep the plan easy to shorten or cancel. You could choose one movie instead of an all-evening event, bring the meal to them, or arrange a video call with relatives who live far away. Include rest and a clear way to end the visit without awkwardness.

The [American Cancer Society's guidance on being a friend to someone with cancer](https://www.cancer.org/cancer/caregivers/how-to-be-a-friend-to-someone-with-cancer.html) encourages listening, practical help, and visits shaped around the person's needs.

## Be Flexible With Traditions

Helping someone with cancer during Christmas or another holiday may mean adjusting a tradition because of energy, health, weather, travel ability, or treatment.

Ask which part of the tradition matters most. If baking together is the favorite part, someone else could buy the ingredients and handle cleanup. If a family gathering involves a long drive, a smaller visit closer to home may work better. If an evening celebration is tiring, consider an earlier time.

A meaningful holiday does not need to look the same as previous years. Let the person decide what to keep, change, or skip, without asking them to explain or apologize.

The [National Cancer Institute's holiday coping tips](https://www.cancer.gov/rare-brain-spine-tumor/blog/2018/coping-with-holidays) offer additional ideas for adjusting plans and staying connected when in-person activities are difficult.

## What About Gifts?

The best gifts for adults with cancer reflect the individual: their tastes, interests, routines, and what they would actually use. Their diagnosis does not have to be the theme of the gift.

Thoughtful options may include:

- A comfortable item they have said they would enjoy.
- Favorite foods, if appropriate for their preferences and care guidance.
- An experience with flexible dates and easy cancellation.
- A family photo or a small album, with permission from those pictured.
- A book, puzzle, craft supply, or something related to a hobby.
- Gift cards for meals or transportation services available in their area.
- Help with a task they would otherwise need to manage.

Before purchasing something expensive or booking an outing, ask. Check accessibility, transportation, scheduling, and whether someone will need to accompany them. Choose gifts that do not create extra work, storage problems, or pressure to participate.

You do not need to select medical products or offer treatment advice to give a useful gift.

## Help Caregivers Too

Caregivers may be balancing work, appointments, household responsibilities, and holiday plans. Supporting them can make daily life easier for both the caregiver and the person with cancer.

Offer a meal, an errand, transportation, or help coordinating family communication. With everyone's agreement, you might spend time with the person while the caregiver takes a break, provided their needs are within what you can safely handle. Confirm any necessary arrangements in advance.

Ask the caregiver what would help them personally, too. "Could I take care of dinner Tuesday so you have one less thing to plan?" is a useful place to start. The [National Cancer Institute's caregiver resources](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/coping/caregiver-support) provide further information about support for families and friends.

## When a Meaningful Wish or Experience May Help

Some adults facing Stage IV, advanced, or metastatic cancer may want to create a larger meaningful experience with loved ones. That might be a special family gathering, a favorite outing, or time together somewhere that matters to them.

One More Moment helps eligible adults in Michigan create meaningful Moments with the people they love. It is one possible source of cancer support in Michigan, alongside the everyday help offered by friends, family, caregivers, and community members.

Learn more about [adult cancer wishes in Michigan](/adult-cancer-wishes-michigan/) and whether a request may be appropriate. Eligibility, health, timing, logistics, and available resources affect what may be possible. A request does not guarantee approval or a particular experience.

One More Moment is an independent Michigan nonprofit and is not affiliated with Make-A-Wish.

## Start With One Helpful Offer

If you are deciding what to do for someone with cancer during the holidays, choose one practical offer and ask whether it would be welcome. Listen to the answer, agree on the details, and leave room for plans to change.

For an adult in Michigan facing Stage IV, advanced, or metastatic cancer who wants a meaningful experience with loved ones, you can also [Request a Moment](/moments/#request-a-moment). Include the person's wishes in the conversation whenever possible, and reach out early if holiday timing matters.

Whether or not a Moment is requested, a thoughtful offer can help someone spend more of the season doing what matters to them.
